Blue Ridge · 3,000 ft

One slope, seven hundred miles

Everything east of the Appalachian crest drains the same direction. Ridge to Coast follows that slope — through hard crystalline rock, across the Fall Line, and out over the sediment of the coastal plain — and tells you what it means for the ground you actually stand on.

The Fall Line is the whole story

Run a finger down the map from Peekskill through Philadelphia, Washington, Richmond, Raleigh, Columbia, and Augusta. Those cities sit on a line, and it is not a coincidence. It is where the ancient hard rock of the Piedmont meets the soft sediment of the coastal plain. Rivers crossing that seam drop over rapids — which stopped boats, and turned mills.

Every one of those cities was founded at the head of navigation, at the last point a ship could reach and the first point a river could do work. Two hundred years later the seam still decides your soil, your drainage, and how early your ground warms in spring.

What the map tells you

  • Where you are, ecologically. EPA Level III ecoregion, physiographic province, and the nearest corridor city.
  • What will survive. USDA hardiness zone at your location, with native plants and invasive species for your region.
  • When to plant. A month-by-month calendar for your zone, plus live frost advisories from the National Weather Service.
  • What the water is doing. Current USGS streamflow on the rivers near you.
  • What is out there right now. Recent iNaturalist observations in your ecoregion.

Who it is for

Gardeners deciding what to put in the ground this month. Growers reading frost risk. Arborists matching species to site. Watershed stewards and conservation volunteers who need to know which side of the seam a parcel sits on. Anyone who has wondered why the soil changes halfway across their county.

Where the data comes from

Ecoregions
US EPA Level III ecoregions, refreshed from the EPA's published dataset.
Hardiness
USDA Plant Hardiness Zone Map, zones 3b–10a across 22 states.
Fall Line
Hand-traced from published USGS surveys. Approximate by nature — it is a transition, not a fence.
Frost
National Weather Service advisories, fetched live for your location.
Streamflow
USGS Water Services, current gauge readings.
Observations
iNaturalist, recent research-grade records per ecoregion.
Basemap
OpenStreetMap contributors, rendered by CARTO. Community gardens from OpenStreetMap.

How it is built

The map is a static page. No tracking, no analytics, no accounts, no third-party scripts — the content security policy blocks them outright. Your saved settings stay in your own browser and are never sent anywhere; you can read and erase all of them on the preferences page.

Live data is fetched straight from the public agencies that publish it, or through a thin caching API that exists only to be polite to those services.
